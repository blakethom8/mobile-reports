Executive Summary

The Short Version

The geotechnical report from CRS Engineering reveals a site that is more complex than a flat-lot build, but well within the range of what experienced Orinda hillside contractors handle routinely. The key findings — expansive clay soils, drilled pier foundations, imported non-expansive fill, and subsurface water seepage — are characteristic of the East Bay hills geology and represent known, solvable engineering challenges.

This is not a "stop the project" situation. It is a "price it correctly and manage it carefully" situation. The estimated cost premium over a comparable flat-lot home ranges from $550K–$1.2M, with the most likely scenario around $800K–$900K. One meaningful risk remains open: the slope stability question was not fully evaluated, and closing that gap proactively is the single most important next step.

Overall Assessment

All conditions identified are understood and have industry-standard solutions. The foundation system (drilled piers to bedrock) is the standard approach for this geology. The cost premiums are real and quantifiable. The project is feasible within the stated $5.8M budget if it was modeled with hillside complexity in mind.

Foundation System

What Gets Built Underground

CRS recommends drilled pier (caisson) foundations — vertical concrete columns drilled down through the clay and embedded into bedrock. These are connected at the top by continuous concrete grade beams, which carry the structural loads of the house. This is not an unusual call: drilled piers to bedrock are the industry standard for the East Bay hills.

Drilled Pier Specifications
  • Minimum diameter: 16 inches
  • Minimum pier length: 10 feet
  • Bedrock embedment: 3 feet minimum
  • Minimum pier spacing: 3× pier diameter
  • Allowable skin friction: 450 psf (in bedrock)
  • Grade beam uplift design: 1,000 psf
  • Settlement (total): ≤ 1 inch
  • Settlement (differential): ≤ ½ inch / 50 ft
⚠️ Water Complicates Pier Construction

Because of active subsurface seepage, piers must be built using a "drill, set, and pour" method rather than the simpler standard approach. If more than 6 inches of water pools in the pier hole, concrete must be placed by tremie (a bottom-up underwater pour), and steel casing may be required to prevent bore hole collapse. Each of these adds cost and time per pier.

Grade Beams

All piers are connected with continuous concrete grade beams — the structural frame that ties the foundation together. Grade beams must extend minimum 12 inches below finished grade and are designed for 1,000 psf uplift force (the expansive soil pushing up seasonally). Grade beams carry the full structural load from the house; they get zero support from the soil itself.

🚛 Earthwork & Import Fill
+$200K – $500K premium

This is the biggest wildcard cost on the project and the most important number to nail down with your civil engineer before finalizing the budget.

The problem: all native soil excavated during grading must be hauled off-site and disposed of, because it fails the non-expansive test. Simultaneously, all fill used to build up the pad must be imported from an approved non-expansive source. You're paying twice — once to export your soil, once to import better soil — instead of simply re-grading with what's already there.

  • Native soil export (10-ft cuts): ~$40–$60/CY to haul and dump in Bay Area. Estimated volume: 1,500–3,000 CY depending on final grading plan. Cost: $60K–$180K.
  • Import non-expansive fill (8-ft fills): ~$50–$75/CY delivered (Bay Area premium). Estimated volume: 1,500–3,500 CY. Material cost: $75K–$260K.
  • Keying & benching labor premium: All fill on slopes requires step-cuts into the existing hillside per CRS spec. Adds ~30–40% to raw grading cost. Premium: $30K–$60K.
  • Subdrain installation: Required at all keyways and benches — 4" perforated pipe in 12" gravel bed, with solid outlet lines. ~$15K–$40K.
  • Old foundation removal: Unknown scope until excavation begins. Budget: $15K–$40K contingency.
  • Compaction testing & moisture conditioning: Required at each lift; adds cost and schedule dependencies. ~$10K–$20K premium.
⚠️ Action Required

Get your civil engineer to run fill volume calculations against the grading plan before finalizing the budget. This line item has the widest range of any cost driver and is most likely to be underestimated.

💧 Site Drainage Systems
+$30K – $80K premium

CRS is emphatic: drainage is "an integral part of the performance of the recommended foundations" and foundations can be "seriously damaged" if neglected. Given the convergent valley drainage and active subsurface seepage, your drainage system must go well beyond what a typical flat-lot home requires.

  • Subdrains at all keyways and benches (required)
  • Drainage blankets + perforated pipes at all retaining walls
  • Tight-line downspout connections (no splash blocks adjacent to structure)
  • Site grading to 2–5% slope away from all building perimeters
  • No drainage discharge onto any slopes
  • Existing drainage must be maintained and remain functional during construction
🔬 Geotechnical & Structural Engineering Premiums
+$40K – $80K premium

Every pier excavation must be observed by the geotechnical engineer. All cut slopes must be inspected. All grading requires compaction testing. This is substantially more engineering involvement than a flat-lot build.

  • Construction-phase geo services (pier obs, grading, compaction): $30K–$60K (vs. $8K–$15K on a flat lot)
  • Structural engineering premium for pier + grade beam design, uplift analysis, retaining wall design, and high seismic forces: $15K–$30K premium over standard structural
  • Civil engineering for grading plan, drainage design, subdrain layout: included in above range
⚠️ Risk Contingency (Site-Specific Unknowns)
+$75K – $150K reserve

These are not guaranteed costs, but represent real risks that should be carried as contingency in the budget:

  • Subsurface water complications during pier drilling — If casing and tremie method are required for many or most piers, add $2K–$5K per pier. On 60 piers, that's up to $300K of risk, though typical exposure is $20K–$80K.
  • Old foundation removal — Unknown extent until excavation. Could be minor; could require significant breaking and hauling if foundations are extensive. Budget $15K–$40K.
  • Slope stability investigation & remediation — If triggered by the city or structural engineer, the study alone is $15K–$30K. Any required remediation (soil nails, tiebacks, deeper pier design) could add $150K–$500K. This is the one risk that could meaningfully change the project economics. (See Risk Register.)
  • Soil variability across site — Bedrock depth ranged from 3 ft to 7.5 ft across just 3 test pits. Piers may need to be deeper than designed in some locations, which is discovered and priced in the field.

Timeline Considerations

Schedule Impact vs. a Standard Build

A custom home at your scale in the Bay Area typically takes 18–24 months from groundbreaking to move-in. Your site conditions add time in several specific places:

Pre-Construction Geotech Reviews (+0 weeks) CRS must review final construction drawings and approve the grading plan before work begins. Budget this into design schedule. Typical turnaround: 2–4 weeks per review cycle, but this is design-phase time, not construction-phase.
Site Clearing & Old Foundation Removal (+1–3 weeks) Unknown extent of buried foundations. Discovery happens during clearing. Minor: 1 week. Extensive old foundation: 3 weeks. Schedule buffer needed here before pier drilling can begin.
Earthwork — Keying, Benching, Subdrains (+2–4 weeks premium) Properly keying and benching fill slopes, installing subdrains at each keyway/bench, and coordinating geo engineer attendance for all grading operations takes significantly longer than simple grading. Compaction testing at each lift adds schedule dependencies.
Pier Drilling & Inspection (+2–4 weeks premium) The geotechnical engineer must observe every pier excavation before concrete is poured. With 50–80 piers, this creates a sequential inspection dependency. If subsurface water is encountered in pier holes (likely given TP2/TP3 seepage), tremie pours and potential casing add time per pier. Cure time before grade beam construction: 3–7 days.
Import Fill Procurement (+1–2 weeks) Non-expansive fill must be sourced, sampled, tested, and approved by the geotechnical engineer before it arrives on-site. In the Bay Area, lead time for large volumes of specification fill can run 2–4 weeks. Don't let this be the critical path item.
Subsurface Water Complications Risk (+2–6 weeks if triggered) If water is more extensive than anticipated during pier drilling — requiring engineered dewatering systems or redesign of pier locations — expect 2–6 weeks of unplanned delay. This is a risk item, not a certainty.
Slope Stability Analysis (if required) (+3–9 months) If a formal slope stability analysis is triggered — by the City, the structural engineer, or a subsequent geotechnical review — this must be completed and approved before grading permits can be finalized. Study + peer review + City approval: 3–9 months. This is the single largest potential schedule risk on the project.

Risk Register

Open Items That Could Change the Calculus

All risks below are manageable. They are listed because each has a meaningful potential to expand scope, cost, or timeline if not addressed proactively.

High Priority
Slope Stability — Not Fully Evaluated

CRS explicitly states that deep-seated slope movement "cannot be evaluated" from their investigation (limited to ~10 ft depth), and no slope stability analysis was performed. Mapped slope failures exist within 1 mile north. If the City of Orinda or your structural engineer triggers a formal slope stability requirement, the study takes 3–9 months and potential remediation could add $150K–$500K+. Proactively ask CRS: "Given the convergent drainage and nearby slope failures, should we commission a slope stability analysis now?" It's far cheaper to do it before permit drawings than mid-process.

High Priority
Earthwork Volume — Not Yet Quantified

The budget range for earthwork and import fill ($200K–$500K) has the widest uncertainty of any cost driver. The actual number depends on final grading plan volumes, which haven't been calculated yet. A formal cut/fill analysis from the civil engineer is the single most important pre-budget step. The difference between the low and high case here is $300K. Don't let this be a budget surprise during construction.

Medium Priority
Subsurface Water — Extent Unknown

Water seepage was found near the surface in 2 of 3 test pits. The report acknowledges that groundwater conditions "can and will fluctuate seasonally" and no local groundwater monitoring data exists. During dry season construction, water may be less of an issue. During wet season, it may be more extensive. Make sure the pier drilling schedule accounts for possible wet-season complications, and build tremie/casing contingency into the foundation budget.

Medium Priority
Buried Old Foundations — Unknown Extent

A previous house was removed from the site ~2004–2005. CRS notes that "potentially some existing foundations" remain underground, with a portion overlapping the new house footprint. The scope of removal won't be known until excavation begins. If foundations are extensive or deep, removal cost and schedule impact could be significant. Budget $15K–$40K contingency and keep the schedule flexible at the start of grading.

Medium Priority
Retaining Wall Height Decision

Currently shown at 6 feet (terraced) but may go to 10 feet (non-terraced). This is not just an aesthetic choice — it materially changes structural complexity, foundation demands, drainage requirements, and cost. A 10-ft wall requires higher lateral earth pressure design, larger piers, and more robust drainage than a 6-ft terraced system. Finalize this decision early so structural engineering isn't redone mid-process.

Low Priority
Bedrock Depth Variability

Bedrock was encountered at 3 ft in TP1 but not until 7.5 ft in TP2 — a 4.5-foot variation across the site. Individual piers may need to be drilled deeper than designed when variable conditions are encountered in the field. This is a minor, normal uncertainty for this type of geology, but worth noting for the GC's field management plan.

Low Priority
Report Expiration — March 2028

CRS states the report "should not be used" after 2 years from its date (March 27, 2026). If construction extends beyond March 2028 or permits aren't issued before then, an updated geotechnical report may be required. On a July 2026 permits / Fall 2028 move-in schedule, you're right at the boundary. Coordinate with CRS on what "expiration" means practically (update vs. new investigation).

## What We Heard (The Five Truths)

### 1. The buyer is exhausted, not excited
Sarah doesn't want to be "empowered." She wants relief. Every incumbent brand either infantilizes the parent (cutesy senior imagery) or gamifies the caregiver (productivity SaaS energy). The winning emotional register is **a calm hand on the shoulder** — competence without hype.

### 2. The family is the unit, and the family is tense
The product's real competitor is the group text and the resentment inside it. Mike doesn't need more notifications; he needs *shared ground truth* so he can trust Sarah without auditing her. Brand language must be **plural** — "your family," "together," "everyone sees the same picture" — never "you, the caregiver."

### 3. The parent must keep her dignity
Ruth's line — "I'm not a project" — is the moral constraint on the whole brand. Consent, agency, and dignity must be visible in the product (permission screens, consent flows) and in the marketing (never depict the parent as a burden or a data source). This is also our differentiation: nobody in the category markets *to the parent's dignity*.

### 4. Trust is the product; humans are the proof
Dana and Elena converge: the AI does the legwork, but the *reason families hand over bank credentials and MyChart access* is the human onboarding specialist and the visible security posture. Every deliverable — ad, landing page, login screen — must carry the human+AI pairing and the trust signals (HIPAA, bank-level encryption, audit trails, named specialist).

### 5. The brand must work up-market and across the chain
Marcus (health plan) needs something he can put in a member benefits packet. Advisors (elder law, wealth managers) need something they're proud to refer. Sarah needs something approachable at $79–149/mo. One brand system must flex from D2C warmth to B2B2C credibility — or we need to know which direction we're choosing.

## Decisions Coming Out of the Meeting

1. **Explore three distinct brand directions** before committing (see `01-brand-strategy.md`):
   - **Direction A — "Harbor"**: warm, dignified, human-first. The calm hand on the shoulder.
   - **Direction B — "Trellis"**: modern, confident, operational. The eldercare operating system / family chief of staff. The "cool" one.
   - **Direction C — "Meridian"**: premium, quiet-luxury concierge. Built for the affluent ICP and advisor/health-plan channel.
2. **Each direction gets a full expression**: marketing website, advertising suite (display, social, email), and voice/tone guide baked into the copy.
3. **Product definitions written from every seat in the chain**: primary coordinator, remote sibling, parent, FSR, health plan buyer.
4. **Account, login, payment, and permission flows get designed now** — because Elena is right: trust is visual before it's contractual, and these "boring" screens are where trust is won or lost.
5. **Pricing surfaces as published tiers** (Connected $79 / Supported $149 / Managed $299) with the income-scaled/scholarship note preserved — mission-driven, never extractive.

## What We Explicitly Rejected

- Leading with "AI" in the headline (interesting, not trust-building).
- Wellness-app pastels and inspirational vagueness (families need seriousness).
- Marketplace/lead-gen energy (commission suspicion kills trust).
- Marketing to the senior as sole user (the adult children are buyer + operator).
- Promising full automation ("we handle everything") — promise visibility, coordination, legwork reduction, confidence.

# Harbor — Brand Notes (Direction A)

*Companion to `index.html` (marketing site) and `ads.html` (advertising suite). Strategy source: `branding/00-stakeholder-synthesis.md` and `branding/01-brand-strategy.md`.*

---

## The idea in one line

**Harbor is the calm hand on the shoulder** — a place a tired, worried adult child can arrive at, at 11pm after a bad phone call, and feel their pulse slow down.

The name does the work: a harbor is not a rescue and not a machine. It's a *steady place* boats return to, watched over by a light that never goes out. That maps exactly to the product truth — constant, quiet vigilance (the assistant) plus a human keeper of the light (the Family Success Representative).

## Voice & tone

### The register

Reassuring, plain-spoken, emotionally literate. Think: a hospice nurse's steadiness with a good editor's economy. Headspace's warmth, One Medical's competence — never one without the other (warmth alone reads passive; competence alone reads cold).

### Rules of the voice

1. **Short sentences.** Stressed readers can't parse subordinate clauses. If a sentence needs a second comma, split it.
2. **Recognition before proposition.** Every surface names the reader's reality first ("Twelve portals, one mom") and only then introduces Harbor. People trust brands that describe their week accurately.
3. **Plural family language.** "Your family," "everyone sees the same picture," "together." Never "you, the caregiver" — the unit is the family, and the group text is the real competitor.
4. **The parent is a person, not a subject.** Ruth speaks in first person on the site ("I'm not a project"). The parent appears as a decision-maker with a name and a sense of humor, never as a data source, a risk, or a burden.
5. **Absolve, don't accuse.** When naming family friction: "Nobody's wrong — the group text is just a terrible system." Blame the tool, never the sibling. This lowers defensiveness and makes the ad forwardable *inside* the family.
6. **Emotional honesty over positivity.** We say "hard season," "the thick of it," "the messiest part." We never say "journey," "empower," or "stress-free."
7. **Promise legwork, not magic.** Visibility, coordination, fewer surprises, confidence. Never "we handle everything" — over-promising in this category is a trust withdrawal we can't afford.
8. **Permission to say no.** Every conversion moment carries a pressure-release valve ("No rush," "Not the right time? That's completely okay"). In eldercare, the brand that lets you walk away is the one you come back to.

### Words we use / words we don't

| Use | Avoid |
|---|---|
| the legwork, the whole picture, steady, calm, aligned | AI-powered, revolutionary, effortless, seamless |
| your family, together, shared | users, caregiv­ing solution, seniors |
| your parent / Mom / Dad / Ruth | the elderly, loved one (as a euphemistic crutch), patient |
| assistant (that does research and reminders) | AI (standing alone in a headline) |
| talk to a real person | book a demo, start your free trial today! |
| hard season, the thick of it | crisis!!, before it's too late, don't wait |
| consent, her choice, her yes | monitoring, surveillance, tracking Mom |

## Copy do / don't

**Do**
- Lead with the sibling-geography truth ("three cities, one mom") — it's the fastest self-identification trigger in the category.
- Pair every mention of the assistant with the human layer in the same breath ("an assistant that does the legwork and real people who help you carry it").
- Show trust as specifics you can name: a named specialist, quarterly check-ins, audit trails, 256-bit encryption, consent gathered by a person in plain language.
- Use the parent's voice to certify dignity ("Harbor asked me first. That's why I said yes").
- Write CTAs as invitations: "See how Harbor works," "Talk to a real person," "Whenever your family is ready."
- Keep pricing plain and family-priced ("per family, every sibling included, no per-seat math") and always attach the scholarship note — the mission is load-bearing, not decorative.

**Don't**
- Don't lead with "AI" alone — interesting, not trust-building (explicit stakeholder rejection).
- Don't use urgency mechanics: countdowns, "limited spots," fear-forward statistics, red anything.
- Don't depict the parent as a burden, a decline curve, or a to-do item. No frail-hands stock-photo energy, even in illustration.
- Don't gamify or productivity-speak ("optimize your caregiving workflow") — Sarah wants relief, not empowerment.
- Don't use wellness-app vagueness ("peace of mind starts here") without an operational specific in the same section.
- Don't market to the senior as sole user, and don't write as if the coordinator is alone — both erase the family unit.
- Don't promise outcomes we can't own ("never miss anything again"). Promise the watching, the flagging, the follow-through.

## Visual system rationale

### Palette
Cream `#f7f4ee` and soft white `#fffdf9` ground everything — warm paper, not clinical white, not SaaS gray. Sage `#8fae9d` and teal-blue `#7ea9b3` carry calm/health/steadiness; deep trust blue `#4e7a86` is reserved for moments of commitment (CTAs, the mission band, the logo) so it retains authority. Warm sand `#d7c1a6` is the human accent — the lighthouse beam, the quote rules, the scholarship block. Ink `#23313a` and secondary `#5e6d76` keep text contrast accessible on the warm grounds. Sage and sand are **decorative only** — never body text on cream (contrast too low).

### Type
Georgia-stack serif headlines give warmth, gravity, and a faint editorial memory — this could be a letter, not a landing page. System/Inter sans body keeps reading effortless for a wide age range. Large sizes, generous line-height: legibility is an accessibility feature *and* an emotional one (nothing about the page should feel effortful).

### Shape language
Rounded containers (20–28px radii), soft layered cards, generous whitespace. The recurring **wave motif** (footer of banners, billboard base) and the lighthouse mark carry the harbor metaphor quietly — always ambient, never literal-nautical kitsch (no anchors, no ropes, no ship wheels).

### The mark
A simple lighthouse in a deep-blue circle: beams left and right (watching in both directions — toward the parent and toward the family), a teal band on the tower, sage water below. Reads at 16px favicon size and at billboard scale. The beams are sand-colored — human warmth is the light source.

## Key creative decisions (and why)

1. **The hero shows the product's *feeling*, not its UI.** "Ruth's week — All steady" with three handled items is an abstract composition, not a screenshot. At the moment of first contact the buyer needs to feel the exhale; dashboards can come later in the funnel. (Also keeps the parent named and dignified from the very first pixel.)

2. **"Talk to a real person" is the primary conversion action sitewide.** Not "start free trial." The human onboarding *is* the product's trust moat, so the marketing funnel routes through it deliberately. Every CTA anchor on the page lands on the FSR section.

3. **The FSR gets a name, a face, and a quote.** Dana Okafor is illustrated (not stock photography — illustration keeps the promise honest at concept stage and avoids the fake-smiling-agent trope). Her quote does double duty: humanizes the service and repositions the technology as *her* tool ("the technology just makes sure I never miss anything").

4. **The testimonial is a trust-repair story, not a time-saved story.** Sarah/Chicago, Mike/Portland, Ruth/Phoenix. The headline outcome is "we're a team again" and "he caught something I'd missed" — sibling trust restored, with the time savings demoted to a supporting stat card. Ruth gets the joke in the quote, which keeps her a person.

5. **Trust section ends with the parent's voice.** Six credentials (HIPAA, encryption, named specialist, check-ins, audit trail, consent) are table stakes; Ruth's blockquote — "Harbor asked me first" — is the differentiator nobody else in the category can say.

6. **The consent promise is structural, not fine print.** It appears in the hero illustration ("confirmed with Ruth"), step 2 of How It Works, its own trust card, the first FAQ, and the welcome email's step 2. Repetition here is deliberate: dignity is the moral constraint of the whole brand.

7. **Mission band uses the deep palette.** The "one million families" section shifts to deep trust blue — the visual register of permanence and commitment — and explicitly frames the company as built to last decades ("patiently, independently"), echoing the Epic-style long-hold strategy without saying "Epic."

8. **Ads sell recognition, not features.** Each trigger-event social unit opens with an accurate, kind description of the reader's week (post-discharge blur; post-diagnosis fear; group-text resentment) and offers one gentle step. The billboard — "You flew in for the weekend. We never left." — honors the visiting sibling's effort while stating the brand's constancy in seven words.

9. **Emails come from Dana, not "The Harbor Team."** Named sender, reply-to-a-human, subject lines that de-escalate ("no rush"). The nurture email is useful even if the reader never converts — content-as-trust-deposit is the acquisition strategy this brand can defend.

10. **Known risk, managed:** Direction A can read soft/passive. Countermeasures baked in: operational specifics in every section (reconciled med lists, follow-ups within 7 days, audit trails, resolved duplicate charge), concrete numbers in pricing and the story stats, and verbs of action ("handled," "resolved," "tracked to completion") threaded through the calm.

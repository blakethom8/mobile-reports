# Trellis — Brand Notes (Direction B)

*"The family's chief of staff." Companion to `index.html` (marketing site) and `ads.html` (advertising suite). Strategy source: `../00-stakeholder-synthesis.md`, `../01-brand-strategy.md`.*

---

## 1. The idea in one breath

A trellis doesn't grow the garden — it holds it up. The family provides the care; Trellis provides the structure. Everything in this direction flows from that division of labor: we never claim to care *for* the parent, we claim to organize the family that does. That's why the register is operational ("command center," "owners," "briefs," "legwork") while the subject matter stays deeply human. The positioning line — **"From group-text chaos to one command center"** — names the villain (chaos, never a person) and the promise (structure) in nine words.

The competitive posture is deliberate: this is the direction that makes a 45-year-old, tech-comfortable buyer say *"finally, someone serious."* Linear/Notion-level polish signals operational competence in a category full of cutesy senior imagery and wellness pastels — and per the stakeholder research, competence *is* the emotional benefit. Sarah doesn't want to be inspired. She wants staff.

## 2. Voice & tone

**The character:** an exceptionally good chief of staff. Crisp, direct, quietly confident, occasionally funny — and unmistakably on your side. Speaks in verbs, shows receipts, never performs empathy it can demonstrate instead.

**Rules of the voice:**

- **Verbs over adjectives.** Not "powerful, seamless coordination" but "the legwork gets done, the tasks have owners." If a sentence can show the product doing something, it must.
- **Show the product early.** The hero contains a working dashboard mock before the first scroll. Ads carry UI, briefs, signals — never abstract lifestyle imagery. Claims are demonstrated, not asserted ("Every signal cites its sources" ships with a source-chip UI, not a trust adjective).
- **Plural, always.** "Your family," "everyone sees the same picture," "set up your family." Never "you, the caregiver" — the unit is the sibling group plus the parent. (Research truth #2: the family is the unit, and the family is tense.)
- **Concrete over categorical.** "Utility bill up 40%, pharmacy spend down" beats "proactive insights" every time. Numbers, names, days of the week. Fictional families (the Thomsons, the Alvarezes) have real cities, real task lists.
- **Wit is seasoning, not sauce.** One light line per section, maximum, and it must punch at *systems* (spreadsheets, portals, phone trees, the group text), never at people — and absolutely never at the parent. Test: would the joke survive being read aloud at the parent's kitchen table?
- **Say the price of things honestly.** "We can see, never move, money." "Cancel anytime, no phone-tree obstacle course." "We can't undo this call." Directness about limits is the trust strategy.

**Tone dial by context:**

| Context | Setting |
|---|---|
| Homepage, feature sections | Full voice — crisp, witty, product-forward |
| Pricing, security, permissions | Direct and plain; zero wit near money or PHI claims |
| FSR / human-support content | Warmest register; first-person quotes; still concrete |
| Trigger-event moments (post-fall, post-ER) | No wit at all. Acknowledge, reduce the ask, lead with the human. See Social ad 3 |
| The parent, anywhere | Dignity register: she's a member, never a subject. "It's her care." |

## 3. Do / Don't

**Do**

- Lead with the chaos the reader already lives in — group text, portal sprawl, the #REF! spreadsheet — then resolve it visually into structure.
- Name the "unpaid COO" dynamic with empathy ("one sister quietly doing all of it").
- Give every task, alert, and example a named owner (Sarah, Mike, Jenny) — ambiguity is the enemy the product kills.
- Put a named human (Dana, FSRs) within one scroll or one paragraph of every AI claim. Formula: *agents for legwork, humans for trust* — always paired.
- Show trust as artifacts: audit-trail rows, consent pills ("Approved by Ruth"), read-only badges, source citations.
- Keep the mission visible: one million families, scholarships on every tier, income-scaled pricing with "same product, same FSR."
- Use amber for signal (alerts, CTAs, the lattice node), evergreen for ground, moss for support, off-white for air.

**Don't**

- Don't lead with "AI" in a headline. AI appears as staff doing legwork, subordinate to outcomes. ("Agents on staff," never "AI-powered!")
- Don't depict the parent as a burden, a project, a data source, or a punchline. Ruth grants and revokes access; the copy says so.
- Don't blame a sibling. The villain is always the system ("the group text isn't the problem — it's the symptom").
- Don't promise full automation. Promise visibility, coordination, legwork reduction, readiness. Agents "tee up decisions"; people decide. Always.
- Don't use wellness-pastel softness, stock hands-holding-hands, or inspirational vagueness.
- Don't use marketplace/lead-gen energy — no urgency timers, no "3 families near you signed up."
- Don't be witty about money, PHI, security, or a crisis. Ever.
- Don't gate warmth by tier — scholarships get the same FSR, and we say so explicitly.

## 4. How "cool without cold" is enforced

This direction's stated risk is that Linear-grade cool turns to ice at the worst moment of someone's life. Six structural guardrails, all visible in the deliverables:

1. **Warm substrate.** The "cool" surfaces sit on warm materials: off-white `#faf8f3` (not clinical white), evergreen (organic, not tech-navy), amber (candlelight, not alarm red). The palette is a garden at dusk wearing a well-cut suit.
2. **Humans are load-bearing, not decorative.** Dana has a name, a face treatment, a direct quote, and a "reply comes straight to me" promise. The FSR section is a full evergreen band, not a support-page footnote. Every AI claim is chaperoned by a human one.
3. **The parent holds the pen.** Ruth appears in the permissions UI as a member with grant/revoke power; the consent pill and the "I'm not a project" ethic are rendered *in the product*, not just claimed in copy. Dignity is shown as a feature, which is the coolest possible way to be warm.
4. **Wit has a curfew.** The tone dial (above) turns humor off precisely where users are most fragile: pricing, security, trigger events, anything near the parent. Social ad 3 is the proof case — same brand, zero jokes, human-first CTA ("Talk to a person").
5. **Operational language, emotional payloads.** The crispest lines carry the most feeling: "The group text can go back to being about the grandkids." "The next call, your whole family will be ready for." Structure is the syntax; relief is the meaning.
6. **The mission stays priced in.** Scholarships appear inside the pricing section (not a buried footer link), with the explicit note that reduced-price families get the identical product and FSR. Cool brands exclude; Trellis's cool is the confidence to include.

## 5. Visual system (as executed)

- **Palette:** deep evergreen `#1d3b2f` (ground/authority), warm off-white `#faf8f3` (air), moss `#6f8f7a` (support/secondary), amber `#d9a441` (signal/CTA/the node on the lattice), charcoal ink `#20242a` (text). Accessible-contrast pairings only; moss never carries small text on light ground.
- **Logomark:** a rounded square holding a diamond lattice — the trellis — with a single amber node at the crossing point (the family at the center of the structure). Rotates 45° on hover: the one place the logo is allowed to be playful.
- **Type:** geometric system sans throughout; display sizes at −0.03em tracking; mono for audit trails, timestamps, and step numbers (the "receipts" typeface).
- **Layout:** structured 12-col grids, visible product UI in nearly every section, alternating light/evergreen bands for rhythm. The lattice appears as a 45°-rotated background pattern in the hero and mission band — structure, literally, behind everything.
- **Motion:** CSS-only and gentle — rise-on-load in the hero, hover lifts on cards, underline draws in the nav, task rows that nudge 2px. All wrapped in `prefers-reduced-motion` guards. Motion says "alive and maintained," never "look at me."
- **Iconography:** inline SVG, 1.4–1.7px strokes, evergreen lines with a single amber accent per icon. No emoji in UI surfaces (the group-text mock is the deliberate exception — chaos gets to look like chaos).

## 6. Creative rationale — why this direction can win

- **It converts the actual buyer.** ICP 1 (affluent, distributed, tech-comfortable siblings) evaluates this category with SaaS instincts. Trellis is the only direction that matches how they already judge software — and then surprises them with warmth where incumbents surprise with clip-art.
- **It makes the price legible.** "Chief of staff" frames $79–299/mo as hiring, not subscribing — supported by value math in the copy ("costs less than the flight you book when something goes wrong," "one flag caught early pays for the month").
- **It owns the demo as marketing.** Because the voice rule is *show the product early*, every asset doubles as a product proof: the hero is a dashboard, the nurture email *is* a weekly brief, the comparison table is the sales conversation. Product-led energy compounds; adjectives don't.
- **It survives the evaluation criteria.** Sarah at 11pm gets "talk to a person" and no jokes. Ruth sees herself holding the pen. Marcus gets HIPAA/audit visuals that don't look like a startup toy. The advisor gets a comparison table they can forward. And the system — grids, lattice, evergreen — is trend-proof enough to hold for thirty years.
- **Watch-outs for the bake-off:** B2B2C contexts (Marcus's member packets) may want the wit dialed to zero by default; test the "chief of staff" metaphor with non-corporate audiences (it's flattering to professionals, possibly foreign outside that frame); and keep auditing every new asset against the kitchen-table test — cool is only ever borrowed against warmth being visibly, structurally present.

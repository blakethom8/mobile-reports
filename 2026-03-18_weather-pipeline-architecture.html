🌦️ Weather Pipeline Architecture — How Forecasts Become Bets

Prepared by Chief | March 18, 2026

Plain-English architecture for turning weather information into disciplined Kalshi trading decisions

One-sentence thesis: the system should first estimate reality, then compare that estimate to market prices, then decide whether the edge survives execution. In short: forecast first, bid second.

The Core Mental Model

This platform is not just a weather forecaster, and it is not just a betting bot. It is a decision engine that sits between the real world and the market.

The three-layer architecture of information

  1. Estimate reality — What is the actual probability distribution for tomorrow's weather outcome?
  2. Observe the market — What prices is Kalshi currently offering for those outcomes?
  3. Convert edge into action — Is any mispricing large enough to trade after spread, fees, and fill risk?

That separation matters because these are different questions. Being good at weather prediction is not automatically the same thing as being good at trading. Markets can be noisy, slow, overreactive, or just expensive to enter.

Forecast first, bid second

This is the most important principle in the whole architecture.

  • Forecast layer: our independent view of reality
  • Bid / decision layer: our response to market opportunity

If our model thinks there is a 38% chance of rain tomorrow, that is a belief about the world. If the market is pricing the rain contract at 24%, that is a pricing opportunity. Those are related, but they are not the same thing.

Why this is healthy: the system can produce a perfectly good forecast even when there is no trade. That keeps us from forcing action just because we built a model.

What Is the Atomic Unit of Analysis?

The cleanest way to structure the ML dataset is:

one row = one contract × one timestamp

Every row should answer the same questions:

  • Which contract is this?
  • What time are we evaluating it?
  • What was the current market price?
  • What was the latest available forecast?
  • What did our model think fair probability was?
  • What was the implied edge?
  • Would this have been an actual trade?

The right mental model here is not a static spreadsheet. It is a movie. Each row is one frame in the movie of a market evolving through time.

Why Sibling Buckets Matter

Weather contracts usually come in families:

  • 65–69°F
  • 70–74°F
  • 75–79°F
  • 80–84°F

These are not four unrelated bets. They are four slices of the same underlying probability distribution.

Right question vs wrong question

Wrong: “Is bucket 75–79 good?”

Right: “How is probability distributed across the full set of possible outcomes, and where does the market disagree with that shape?”

If our weather view shifts upward, probability mass doesn’t appear from nowhere. It moves from one bucket into adjacent buckets. That means our system should think in terms of distribution shape, not isolated binary contracts.

Why this changes the ML structure

The model should often work in two steps:

  1. Estimate the full weather distribution at the event level
  2. Map that distribution into fair probabilities for each contract

That is much more natural than training a separate “yes/no” brain for every bucket.

Fair Probability vs Market Price

Another distinction that sounds subtle but matters a lot:

ConceptMeaning
Fair probabilityWhat our system thinks the true chance of the outcome is
Market midThe rough midpoint of current market pricing
Tradable priceThe actual buy or sell price we can hit right now

A contract with a 60% fair probability is not automatically a buy. It only becomes attractive if the tradable price is meaningfully lower than 60%, and lower by enough to survive friction.

Important: midpoint fantasies make bad backtests. The system must compare fair value to executable price, not just to a pretty chart price.

Mispricing Is Not the Same as Price Movement

This is another important distinction for how we design the pipeline.

QuestionWhat it meansWhy we care
Is this contract mispriced?Fair probability is different from market priceGood for value selection
Will this price move soon?Market may reprice in the next 15/60/180 minutesGood for timing entries and exits
Will this settle YES?Final contract outcomeGood for calibration and truth evaluation

A contract can be genuinely mispriced and still not move for hours. A contract can also move sharply even if the underlying weather truth barely changes. That means our system should track multiple targets, not just one:

  • Final resolution target — what actually happened
  • Mispricing target — fair value vs market
  • Timing target — short-horizon market movement

Why Execution Matters

Execution is where theoretical edge either becomes money or dies.

Real markets have:

  • spreads
  • fees
  • thin liquidity
  • partial fills
  • quote staleness
  • time pressure near settlement
Forecast edge → Quoted edge → Executable edge → Realized edge

That funnel is crucial. Every stage shrinks the apparent advantage. The platform has to treat that as a feature, not an inconvenience.

The practical consequence

Even if our weather model is “right,” we still need to ask:

  • Can we buy or sell at a real price?
  • Can we size into it?
  • Does the edge survive fees and spread?
  • Is there enough time left for price convergence or settlement capture?

What Data Stack Makes Sense First?

The best MVP stack is a two-track setup:

NeedRecommended sourceWhy
Forecasting / signal generationOpen-Meteo ensembleFast to build, gives probability-friendly forecast data
Short-range refinementOpen-Meteo GFS + HRRRBetter near-expiry detail for US weather
Official contextNWS APIUseful for station lookup, forecast context, validation
Settlement / truthExact NWS Daily Climate Report / station source Kalshi usesAvoids backtest fraud via source mismatch
MVP source principle: use Open-Meteo to forecast, but use the exact NWS station-based settlement source to judge truth.

Why this matters

If we forecast off one city-level weather source but resolve off a different airport or climate station, we create fake signal and noisy labels. That is one of the easiest ways to build a model that looks smart on paper and dumb in production.

A Simple Example

Suppose tomorrow’s high temperature market for New York has four sibling buckets:

  • 65–69°F
  • 70–74°F
  • 75–79°F
  • 80–84°F

Our weather engine might produce this distribution:

That still doesn’t mean we instantly trade. We would next ask:

  • What is the actual bid/ask on the contracts we want?
  • Is there enough liquidity?
  • Are forecast sources agreeing or diverging?
  • How close are we to settlement?

Only then does the system decide whether the edge is real enough to act on.

How ML Fits In

ML should not be treated like magic sauce on top of a messy workflow. It has a very specific job inside this architecture.

Three useful modeling jobs

  1. Truth estimation — estimate the fair distribution of real-world weather outcomes
  2. Mispricing estimation — quantify how far the market is from fair value
  3. Timing estimation — predict whether the market is likely to reprice soon

That gives us a more realistic sequence:

forecast model → fair contract probabilities → edge model → execution filter

Phased Expansion

We should not cram surf reports, sentiment, news, and every imaginable feature into the first version.

PhaseWhat we addWhy
1. Core weatherForecasts, settlement truth, contract parsing, market snapshotsEstablish a clean baseline
2. Distribution-aware layerSibling bucket normalization, strip-shape featuresRepresent the market correctly
3. Execution-aware layerSpread, liquidity, fill assumptions, feesMake backtests honest
4. Richer external signalsAdditional models, revisions, maybe local context featuresExpand only after baseline is trustworthy
Good discipline: add one family of features at a time and make it earn its keep. Otherwise we end up with feature soup and fake confidence.

The System We Are Actually Building

In plain English, this platform is trying to do one thing well:

Final framing

Build a disciplined pipeline that turns weather information into a fair probability distribution, maps that distribution into contract prices, compares those prices to Kalshi’s market, and only trades when the edge survives real-world execution.

That is the architecture of information. It is also the architecture of restraint: the platform should help us know when not to bet.

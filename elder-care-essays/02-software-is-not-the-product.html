Software Is Not the Product

We keep asking whether software is enough. It's the wrong question — and the right one changes what company we're building.

A working memo for Spencer — on what code can and cannot do in this market.

We are software people, so our reflex is to reach for software. When the first memo argued that pure visibility can't capture this market, the instinct is to ask: fine, but is software enough? I've come to think that's the wrong question, and that asking it better is the most important move we can make right now. Because "just software" means three completely different things, and only one of them is a real business for us — while a different one is the trap we're standing next to.

The question that actually matters is not is software enough. It's what job is the software doing, and who is holding the trust while it does it.

Three things people mean by "just software"

When someone says we could do this as pure software, they could mean any of three businesses, and we should never let the phrase stay ambiguous:

"Just software" — disambiguated
  1. Sold to families. A subscription app that aggregates, summarizes, coordinates. The vision as originally drawn.
  2. Sold to the services businesses. Tooling for the care managers, home-care agencies, and roll-ups — picks and shovels.
  3. Software plus embedded humans, sold to payers/employers. The Wellthy shape: software leverage under a light human layer, distributed through benefits.

Notice that only the first is truly "just software sold to families" — and it is the one with the worst evidence in the entire market.

Why software-to-families keeps dying

Four reasons, and they compound. First, the money in this market has labor attached — every scaled dollar flows to someone who does something, not to a dashboard. Second, the job is labor reduction, not information; the twenty-five weekly hours are errands and phone calls, and visibility barely dents them. Third, trust has to sit on someone's shoulders, and software has none — a family hands bank and medical credentials to a person they've come to trust, not to an app they installed three weeks ago. Fourth, the retrieval layer is the first thing general AI eats; if our software is the aggregation, our moat is a feature with a short shelf life.

Stacked together, software-to-families needs four separate things to break its way at once. Needing all four is not a plan. It's a wish.

The honest steelman

There is one version of "just software" that survives the bear case: sell the software to the services businesses instead of to the families. Be the operating system for the care managers and agencies — the second definition above. It's real software, with real willingness to pay, because those businesses are starving for systems, and you never carry a caregiver payroll.

But look hard at its ceiling. The buyers are small, fragmented, and famously tech-skeptical; the contract sizes are modest; the sales cycles are slow. And most important: you don't hold the family relationship, which was supposed to be the whole moat. You'd be a competent B2B SaaS company selling into a thin market — a fine outcome, but not the company our vision documents describe, and not one with a defensible trust layer. Worth knowing that door exists. Worth being honest that walking through it is a different, smaller ambition.

The reframe that resolves it

Stop asking whether software is enough and ask who holds the trust and who does the job. Today, software can do neither — but it can make the human who does both dramatically more productive. That single reframe moves software from product to leverage, and leverage is where the value has been hiding the whole time.

A geriatric care manager carries around forty families, because coordination is manual — phone calls, portals, faxes, notes. Now suppose our software takes the retrieval, the drafting, the monitoring, the paperwork off her desk, and she can carry a hundred families at the same quality.

You didn't sell her software. You changed the unit economics of a services business — and that is a far larger and far more defensible thing to have done.

This is the part the Arosa story quietly reveals, and the next memo takes it up in full. Arosa proved you can roll up care-management-plus-home-care into a national platform. It did not prove that software changes the caseload math. Nobody has built the care business where software is the reason the humans are three times more efficient. That specific white space — services with genuine software leverage — is unclaimed, and it's defensible in a way neither pure model is: the care managers can't build the software, and the pure-software startups can't manufacture the trust and the labor.

The uncomfortable corollary

If this is right, then the company we should build is a services company with a software margin engine — not a software company with a services garnish. The order of those words is the whole strategy. It decides who we hire first, what we're proud of, what we tell investors, and what we do on a Tuesday.

And it forces a question I don't want us to dodge: if what you and I want in our hearts is to build a software company — clean margins, no payroll of caregivers, a product that scales while we sleep — then this market may be telling us something we don't want to hear. That's not a reason to abandon it. It's a reason to choose it with our eyes open, rather than backing into a services business while telling ourselves it's SaaS.

So: software is not the product. Software is the wedge — and it's only powerful bolted onto trust and labor that a real business already owns. Which raises the obvious next question. If we can't manufacture trust and labor cheaply from nothing, is there a door that lets us buy them?

The Case Against Ourselves

We just built three beautiful brands. That is precisely the moment to write down why none of it might matter.

A working memo for Spencer — the strongest version of the bear case, on purpose.

There is a specific danger in good materials. Last week we produced three complete brand worlds — Harbor, Trellis, Meridian — each with a marketing site polished enough to make you feel the company already exists. It does not. What exists is a conviction and a stack of research, and the research is persuasive in the way that all pre-mortem research is persuasive: it was written by people who wanted the answer to be yes. So before we spend another dollar or another month, I want to write the honest thing. Not a list of risks to manage. The actual case for why this fails.

Read it as a friend would, not a skeptic. If the idea survives this, it is stronger. If it doesn't, we learned it cheaply.

Pain is real. The purchase is not.

Everyone we talk to nods. The pain of managing an aging parent from three time zones away is genuine, widespread, and getting worse with the demographics. But nodding is not paying, and this category has a graveyard that proves the gap. CareZone raised enormous money for almost exactly our aggregation thesis — put the meds, the documents, the money in one place for family caregivers — and ended in an asset sale. People used it. They didn't pay. Wellthy, the strongest living comparable, quietly gave up on selling to families and survives on the employer channel. The pattern across a decade is merciless and consistent: the people with the pain hesitate to pay a subscription, and the people who will pay — employers, health plans — are buying outcomes, not family software.

The counter-evidence is not that no one pays. It's what they pay for.

Because people do pay in this market — a lot. Geriatric care managers bill $150 to $250 an hour and families pay it. Elder law attorneys, daily money managers, in-home aides: real dollars, real willingness. Notice the common thread. Every dollar is attached to a human who does something and takes responsibility for it. The willingness-to-pay evidence in eldercare points at labor, not at visibility. Our $149 tier sits in an unproven middle — too expensive to be an impulse app, too passive to feel like hired help.

The crisis window closes

The trigger events that create urgency — a fall, a diagnosis, a hospitalization — are the same events that pass. Mom stabilizes. Discharge is done. The panic recedes, and the subscription bought in the spike gets re-examined in the calm. We modeled voluntary churn against concierge medicine, but concierge medicine is a doctor you keep seeing. Ours may not feel continuous once the fire is out. Crisis-acquired customers may simply be structurally high-churn customers, and we should assume that until the data says otherwise.

Aggregation is not the job

Here is the uncomfortable one. Our own competitive research admits it in a single line — "the problem is not just retrieval; it is translation into action" — and then the rest of the plan quietly forgets it. A caregiver spends around twenty-five hours a week. That time is driving to appointments, arguing with insurers, managing aides, worrying at 2am. A dashboard removes maybe two or three hours of not-knowing. It does not touch the twenty-two hours of doing. The renewal-day question we cannot yet answer honestly: what does Sarah actually stop doing in month two because we exist? If the answer is "checking portals," we saved her a tenth of her burden and she will feel that when the card is charged.

The commoditization comes from below

Nobody in our documents has said this out loud, so I will. General-purpose AI assistants are getting good, fast. Within our own build horizon, a family's twenty-dollar general agent may plausibly log into the portals with the user present, summarize the records, draft the note to the cardiologist, and produce the weekly family brief. That is our Phase 1 and Phase 2 value. The aggregation-and-summary layer is the most commoditizable part of the entire stack. If our software is the retrieval, our moat is a feature that gets absorbed into a general tool we don't control.

The wedge may be thinner than the deck

The whole positioning rests on distributed siblings collaborating inside a shared workspace. The likelier default — well documented in caregiving research — is that one sibling does everything and the others want, at most, a monthly summary. If real usage is 1.2 active members per family, the collaboration features go unused, the "transparency repairs the family" story loses its mechanism, and our signature cost-splitting feature has almost no one to split across. We wrote our own kill signal into the MVP plan: fewer than two weekly-active members per household at week eight. I think that is the existential experiment, and it comes before anything clever about AI.

And there is a veto we designed in on purpose. Consent-first is morally right and a real differentiator — but it hands a veto to the one person with the least incentive to adopt: the parent. Some meaningful fraction of otherwise-sold families will die at the "ask Mom" step. We have no estimate of that fraction. We should get one.

The arithmetic that quietly kills the north star

Our churn analysis is honest: 2.5% monthly, and most of it structural — care journeys end, and no amount of product quality prevents a death. What nobody did was multiply that against the mission.

50,000 families1,250 / month
250,000 families6,250 / month
1,000,000 families25,000 / month

Our aggressive Year-3 scenario is roughly 150 to 250 new families a month. "Help one million families" is therefore achievable only as a cumulative, decades-long count — or through a channel that enrolls tens of thousands of families a month, which is a different company than the white-glove one we've been designing. Both cannot be the same brand, product, and cost structure at once. And because customers structurally exit in about thirty-three months, our acquisition spend never amortizes into a stable base. Growth marketing becomes a permanent treadmill whose replacement pipeline is, bluntly, grieving families.

Two tensions sit under everything, and they are not footnotes. They are the venture.

Moat versus margin. The humans who create the trust are the costs that cap the margins. Every "humans when it matters" promise is a $125,000-a-year salary, forever. The high-margin tier — AI only — is the exact tier our own validation doubts anyone will buy without a human. If the human is required, margins compress toward a services business. If it isn't, the moat thins toward commoditizable software. That tension is unresolved in the model, which currently hopes for both.

Mission versus math. The churn that is structurally guaranteed makes a million active families arithmetically implausible without a payer-scale channel we have not built and have barely scoped.

What this memo is not

It is not a recommendation to stop. The demographic tailwind is real for thirty years. The white space — one place across medical, physical, and financial care, with a trust layer strong enough to touch both money and health — is genuinely unoccupied, and it's unoccupied for hard reasons, not because no one thought of it. The founding motivation is sound.

What I'm asking is narrower and more useful: that we stop treating these as risks to be smoothed over in a deck, and start treating the two tensions as the central design problem of the company. Resolve moat versus margin and mission versus math deliberately, on purpose, with evidence — and the rest of this list becomes ordinary engineering and go-to-market work. Leave them unresolved and the beautiful brands are just expensive set dressing.

The next two memos take up where this one leaves off. If the need is real but pure visibility can't capture it, then what can software actually do here — and is there a door into this market that hands us the trust and the labor instead of asking us to manufacture both from nothing?
